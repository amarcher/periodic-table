# Superheavy Element Video Specs (91, 95–118)

Production spec for the 25 elements that have no video in `src/data/videoData.ts`.
Doubles as source copy for the element pages.

**Why these 25 are a separate track.** Every other element got a "dramatic reaction"
video. None of these can: each one is either too rare to film or has never existed in a
quantity a camera could see. But GA4 (90 days, Jun–Sep 2026) shows they get real traffic —
237 pageviews — and pay it off badly: **12.1 s of engagement per view, against 25.6 s on
pages that have a video.** Lawrencium is the 5th most-visited element page on the site.

## The premise to avoid

These are **not** made in hadron colliders. The LHC smashes protons at 13 TeV to break
matter into fragments. Superheavy synthesis uses heavy-ion linacs and cyclotrons at roughly
5 MeV per nucleon — about a million times gentler per particle — and the goal is the
opposite: get two nuclei to *barely touch and stick*.

Too slow and they bounce off their mutual electrostatic repulsion. Too fast and the merged
nucleus is so hot it fissions instantly. The usable window is a few MeV wide and it is
**different for every beam–target pairing**. That is the kid-facing hook, and it is also
the reason each element has its own story: it's a docking maneuver, not a demolition derby.

## Visual grammar: six eras, running left to right

The bottom rows are not one method. They are a century of changing technique, and the
technique changes roughly in atomic-number order — so clicking across becomes an arc.

| Era | Elements | Method | Visual register |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Extraction | Pa (91) | Pulled from uranium ore | Industrial chemistry, tonnes of waste |
| 2. Reactor breeding | Am, Cm, Bk, Cf (95–98) | Neutron capture in reactors | Hot cells, manipulator arms, blue glow; these are **weighable and commercial** |
| 3. Bomb debris | Es, Fm (99–100) | Thermonuclear test fallout | 1952 Pacific, filter planes, coral |
| 4. Light-ion fusion | Md–Sg (101–106) | Cyclotron, alpha/boron/carbon beams | One atom at a time; Cold War naming wars |
| 5. Cold fusion | Bh–Cn (107–112) | Pb/Bi targets, medium beams, 1n out | GSI Darmstadt, the SHIP separator |
| 6. Hot fusion | Nh–Og (113–118) | Ca-48 onto actinide targets, 3–4n out | Dubna and RIKEN; the rarest targets on Earth |

## The four axes that vary every single time

1. **The target** is a different rare substance each time — and it's the best visual.
   Lead foil for darmstadtium; 22 mg of berkelium for tennessine; californium for oganesson.
2. **The beam ion** changes: Cr-54, Fe-58, Ni-62, Ni-64, Zn-70, and calcium-48.
3. **The lab** changes, and they look nothing alike: Berkeley, GSI Darmstadt, JINR Dubna, RIKEN.
   Six of these elements are *named* for where they were made.
4. **The decay chain** is a literal fingerprint — a unique cascade of alpha decays with its own
   energies and timings, often ending in spontaneous fission. This is how each discovery was
   *proven*, and it is different for every element by construction.

**Axis 4 is the one that guarantees 25 distinct videos.** Animating the real decay cascade
with real timings is unique per element, scientifically honest, rhythmically distinct, and it
shows how we know the thing existed — without faking footage of a single atom.

## Per-element specs

### Era 1 — Extraction

**91 · Protactinium (Pa)** — *not synthetic at all*
- Natural, in uraninite at 0.3–3 ppm; Pa-231 forms from U-235 decay
- Discovered 1917–18 independently by Lise Meitner & Otto Hahn (Germany) and
  Frederick Soddy & John Cranston (UK); IUPAC confirmed 1949
- Pa-231 half-life **32,760 years**
- **Story beat:** in 1961 the UK Atomic Energy Authority processed **60 tonnes of waste**
  through a 12-stage plant to extract **127 g** of 99.9% pure Pa-231, at about **$500,000**.
  That was the world's supply for decades.
- **Visual:** industrial scale in, a thimble out. Use ocean-sediment dating (back 175,000 years)
  as the payoff — it reads clocks in the seafloor.

### Era 2 — Reactor breeding (weighable, and some of it is in your house)

**95 · Americium (Am)**
- Seaborg's team, Berkeley 1944, 60-inch cyclotron, neutron bombardment of plutonium
- Produced today as Pu-239 + 2n → Pu-241 → β⁻ → Am-241; ~100 g per tonne of spent fuel
- Am-241 half-life **432 years**, decaying to Np-237
- **Story beat:** kept secret through the Manhattan Project, then announced publicly in
  November 1945 **on a children's radio quiz show** — Seaborg told kids before he told scientists.
- **Visual:** the smoke detector. 0.29 µg, ~1 microcurie, alpha particles ionizing air between
  two plates until smoke interrupts the current. *You own some of this element.*

**96 · Curium (Cm)**
- Seaborg, Ralph James, Albert Ghiorso, Berkeley 1944: Pu-239 + He-4 → Cm-242 + n
- Cm-247 half-life **15.6 million years**
- **Story beat:** it **glows purple in the dark** from its own radioactivity.
- **Visual:** Mars. Cm-244 is the alpha source in the APXS instruments flown on Sojourner,
  the Mars Exploration Rovers, and the Philae comet lander — this element has touched rocks
  on another planet and on a comet.

**97 · Berkelium (Bk)**
- Berkeley 1949: Am-241 + He-4 → Bk-243 + 2n
- Bk-249 bred at Oak Ridge HFIR via successive neutron capture (Pu-239 → … → Cm-249 → β⁻ → Bk-249)
- Bk-249 half-life **330 days**; Bk-247 **1,380 years**
- **Story beat:** **just over one gram total has been made at Oak Ridge since 1967**, at roughly
  $1M per batch. It is the raw material for tennessine — see 117.
- **Visual:** the scarcest feedstock on Earth. A single vial that represents six decades of output.

**98 · Californium (Cf)**
- Berkeley 1950: Cm-242 + He-4 → Cf-245 + n — about **5,000 atoms**, 44-minute half-life
- Today Oak Ridge makes ~0.25 g/yr and Russia's RIAR ~0.025 g/yr (2003 figures)
- Cf-251 half-life **898 years**; Cf-252 **2.645 years**
- **Story beat:** Cf-252 is a **commercial product**. One microgram throws off 139 million
  neutrons per minute. It's used for neutron radiography (~77% of use), fuel-rod scanning,
  reactor start-up, oil-well logging, and finding cracks in aircraft.
- **Visual:** a working industrial tool, not a lab curiosity — a neutron source down an oil well.

### Era 3 — Bomb debris

**99 · Einsteinium (Es)**
- Found in the debris of **Ivy Mike**, the first thermonuclear test, Enewetak Atoll, 1 Nov 1952
- Identified December 1952 by Ghiorso with Berkeley, Argonne, and Los Alamos
- **Fewer than 200 atoms** recovered, by ion exchange
- Es-252 half-life **471.7 days**
- **Story beat:** aircraft flew through the mushroom cloud trailing **paper filters**; more was
  later leached from **coral** shipped back from the atoll. Classified until 1955, when Ghiorso
  announced it at the first Geneva Atomic Conference.
- **Visual:** it **glows**, and it puts out about **1,000 watts per gram** of heat. A speck that
  is visibly, dangerously hot.

**100 · Fermium (Fm)**
- Same Ivy Mike debris, found ~2 months after the test; Fm-255 spotted by its 7.1 MeV alphas
- Made by U-238 absorbing neutron after neutron in the instant of the explosion
- Fm-257 half-life **100.5 days**
- **Story beat:** **fermium is the wall.** Past Fm-257, spontaneous fission is so fast that
  neutron capture can't climb any higher — the "fermium gap." Everything above 100 needs a
  completely different technique, which is exactly why the next era looks different.
- **Visual:** the ladder of neutron captures, rung by rung, ending at a step that collapses.
  This is the hinge of the whole bottom-row story — worth making the strongest video of the 25.

### Era 4 — Light-ion fusion, one atom at a time

**101 · Mendelevium (Md)**
- Berkeley 1955, 60-inch cyclotron: an Es-253 target of only **~10⁹ atoms**, hit with alphas
- Md-256, half-life **77.7 min**; **17 atoms** detected. Md-258 half-life 51.6 days
- **Story beat:** the **first isotope of any element ever synthesized one atom at a time.**
- **Visual:** the recoil-catcher trick — the einsteinium sits on the far side of the target so
  that newly made mendelevium atoms *recoil out* and are caught on a **gold foil** behind it.
  A beautifully simple, animatable idea.

**102 · Nobelium (No)**
- Confirmed: Dubna 1966, U-238 + Ne-22 → No-254 + 6n. No-259 half-life **58 min**
- **Story beat:** **the name commemorates a discovery that didn't happen.** Sweden's Nobel
  Institute claimed element 102 in 1957 and named it; the signal turned out to be thorium-225
  contamination and they retracted. Berkeley couldn't reproduce it either — their results were
  fermium isomers. In 1992 IUPAC/IUPAP ruled only Dubna's 1966 work was correct. The Soviet
  team proposed *joliotium*. The wrong name had stuck too hard to change.
- **Visual:** science correcting itself in public, and losing the argument anyway.

**103 · Lawrencium (Lr)**
- Berkeley, **14 Feb 1961**, HILAC: a 3 mg target of three californium isotopes + B-10/B-11;
  ²⁵²Cf + ¹¹B → ²⁵⁸Lr + 5n. Lr-266 half-life **11 hours**
- Disputed with Dubna; IUPAC credited both in 1992, noting only Berkeley's 1971 runs gave
  full confidence
- **Story beat:** in **2015** its first ionization energy was measured at **4.96 eV** — the
  lowest of any lanthanide or actinide — which is the evidence for putting it in group 3 with
  scandium and yttrium rather than in the f-block. **This element's position on the table is
  still argued about.** (Note: it is the most-visited of these 25 pages.)
- **Visual:** the periodic table itself rearranging — the one element whose *address* is contested.

**104 · Rutherfordium (Rf)**
- Dubna 1966–69: Pu-242 + Ne-22. Berkeley 1969: ²⁴⁹Cf + ¹²C → ²⁵⁷Rf + 4n
- Rf-267 half-life **~48 min**
- **Story beat:** opening shot of the **Transfermium Wars**. Soviets proposed *kurchatovium* (Ku),
  Americans *rutherfordium* (Rf). The 1992 working group credited both teams; IUPAC settled on
  rutherfordium only in **1997**, nearly 30 years after the fact.

**105 · Dubnium (Db)**
- Dubna, April 1968: Am-243 + Ne-22. Berkeley, April 1970: ²⁴⁹Cf + ¹⁵N → ²⁶⁰Db + 4n
- Db-268 half-life **~16 hours** (revised 2022) — the longest-lived of any element above 103.
  Db-270 ~1 hour, from only three observed atoms
- **Story beat:** the naming fight at its most absurd — **four** competing names in play
  (*nielsbohrium*, *hahnium*, *joliotium*, *dubnium*) before 1997 resolution.
- **Visual:** a Cold War map with two flags planted on the same square.

**106 · Seaborgium (Sg)**
- Berkeley + LLNL 1974: ²⁴⁹Cf + ¹⁸O → ²⁶³ᵐSg + 4n. Sg-267 half-life **~9.8 min**
- **Story beat:** IUPAC ruled in August 1994 that **no element may be named after a living
  person** — and Glenn Seaborg was alive. He said: *"This would be the first time in history
  that the acknowledged and uncontested discoverers of an element are denied the privilege of
  naming it."* IUPAC reversed itself and approved the name in August 1997. Seaborg died in
  February 1999, about 18 months later.
- **Visual:** a man looking at his own name on the periodic table. Pair with 118.

### Era 5 — Cold fusion at GSI Darmstadt

All six use a lead or bismuth target and evaporate exactly **one** neutron. Same building,
same separator (SHIP), same two scientists — Peter Armbruster and Gottfried Münzenberg —
across fifteen years. Lean into the repetition here; it's a *workshop*, and each element is
one more impossible thing made in it.

**107 · Bohrium (Bh)** — GSI 1981: ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁵⁴Cr → ²⁶²Bh + n. **Five atoms.**
Bh-270 half-life **~2.4 min**. Named for Niels Bohr (originally *nielsbohrium*; shortened 1994).
*Story beat:* in 2000 the Paul Scherrer Institute made a **volatile oxychloride** of it and
measured how it stuck to a surface — chemistry performed on a handful of atoms, confirming it
behaves like a group 7 element.

**108 · Hassium (Hs)** — GSI 1984: ²⁰⁸Pb + ⁵⁸Fe → ²⁶⁵Hs. **Three atoms.**
Hs-271 half-life **46 s**. Named for *Hassia*, Latin for Hesse.
*Story beat:* **Hs-270 is a candidate deformed doubly-magic nucleus** (Z=108, N=162) — a
stability sweet spot that isn't the famous island. *Visual:* hassium tetroxide, volatile,
behaving exactly like osmium's heavier twin.

**109 · Meitnerium (Mt)** — GSI, **29 Aug 1982**: ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁵⁸Fe → ²⁶⁶Mt + n. **One single atom.**
Confirmed at Dubna three years later. Mt-278 half-life **4.5 s**.
*Story beat:* Lise Meitner co-discovered protactinium and co-discovered nuclear fission, and
was passed over for the Nobel. Hers is **the only element named specifically after a
non-mythological woman.** *Visual:* one atom, one woman, one long overdue correction.

**110 · Darmstadtium (Ds)** — GSI, **9 Nov 1994**: ²⁰⁸Pb + ⁶²Ni → ²⁶⁹Ds + n. One atom on the 9th,
two more on the 12th and 17th. Decays by alpha to Hs-265. Ds-281 half-life **~14 s**.
*Story beat:* they nearly named it **wixhausium**, after Wixhausen, the suburb where the
accelerator sits. *Visual:* a very ordinary German suburb that has an element in it.

**111 · Roentgenium (Rg)** — GSI, **8 Dec 1994**: ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁶⁴Ni → ²⁷²Rg + n. Three nuclei, three
more on repeat in 2002. Rg-282 half-life **100 s**. Named for Wilhelm Röntgen.
*Visual:* close the loop — the man who discovered X-rays, honored by an element that can only be
seen by its radiation.

**112 · Copernicium (Cn)** — GSI, **9 Feb 1996**: ²⁰⁸Pb + ⁷⁰Zn → ²⁷⁷Cn + n. One confirmed atom —
**a second was retracted because the data had been fabricated.** Cn-285 half-life **~30 s**.
Named for Copernicus; accepted 19 Feb 2010, the 537th anniversary of his birth.
*Story beat:* the fraud and the self-correction. *Visual:* relativistic effects make it **more
volatile than mercury**, with noble-gas-like behavior — possibly **the first gaseous metal**.

### Era 6 — Hot fusion: calcium-48 and the rarest targets on Earth

**113 · Nihonium (Nh)** — RIKEN, Japan (Kōsuke Morita): ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁷⁰Zn → ²⁷⁸Nh + n
- **Three atoms in nine years.** Bombardment began Sept 2003; first atom July 2004; second
  April 2005; the decisive third in **August 2012 after 450 more days of irradiation**
- Decay chain (fully observed, four alphas): **²⁷⁸Nh → ²⁷⁴Rg → ²⁷⁰Mt → ²⁶⁶Bh → spontaneous
  fission of ²⁶²Db**
- Nh-286 half-life **~10 s**. Priority recognized 2015 — first element discovered in Asia
- **Visual:** the purest "patience" story on the table. Nine years of a machine running for
  three events. This decay chain is fully characterized — use it as the reference animation.

**114 · Flerovium (Fl)** — JINR Dubna, June 1999: ²⁴⁴Pu + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁰Fl + 2n
- First signal Dec 1998: a single atom alpha-decaying after 30.4 s. Fl-289 half-life **~1.9 s**
- **Story beat:** **the island of stability.** Fl-298 (Z=114, N=184) is predicted doubly magic —
  a superheavy that might last far longer than anything yet made. Nobody has reached it.
- **Visual:** chemistry experiments in 2007–08 found it unexpectedly volatile for group 14;
  it sticks to gold the way copernicium does, and may be a gas.

**115 · Moscovium (Mc)** — JINR + LLNL, Aug 2003: ²⁴³Am + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁸⁸Mc + 3n (and ²⁸⁷Mc + 4n)
- Alpha-decays to **nihonium** in about 100 ms; chain terminates at Db-268
- Mc-290 half-life **650 ms**. Named for Moscow Oblast
- **Visual:** the handoff — this is where element 113 comes from when you make it the other way.
  Show the chain running *down* into a neighbor the viewer just visited.

**116 · Livermorium (Lv)** — JINR + LLNL, **19 July 2000**: ²⁴⁸Cm + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹³Lv + 3n
- A single atom, alpha-decaying to flerovium. Lv-293 half-life **~80 ms**
- IUPAC recognition 1 June 2011
- **Story beat:** named for Lawrence Livermore National Lab → the town of Livermore, California →
  **Robert Livermore, a rancher.** The naming chain runs from a superheavy nucleus back to a
  19th-century cattleman.

**117 · Tennessine (Ts)** — JINR + Oak Ridge + LLNL + Vanderbilt, 2010:
²⁴⁹Bk + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁴Ts + 3n (1 event) and → ²⁹³Ts + 4n (5 events). **Six atoms.**
- Ts-294 half-life **~112 ms**; Ts-293 **~21 ms**
- **Story beat — the best one on the table.** The berkelium-249 target was bred at Oak Ridge
  HFIR over a **250-day irradiation** ending late December 2008, yielding **22 mg**. Then 90 days
  cooling, 90 days chemical processing. Then it had to reach Dubna before its **330-day half-life**
  ran it out. **Russian customs rejected the shipment twice for incomplete paperwork. The
  berkelium crossed the Atlantic five times.** It finally entered Russia in June 2009, was laid
  down as a thin film on titanium, and reached Dubna in July 2009.
- **Visual:** a countdown clock against a cargo manifest. The rarest material on Earth, stuck in
  customs, decaying.

**118 · Oganesson (Og)** — JINR + LLNL: ²⁴⁹Cf + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁴Og + 3n
- 2002 (announced 2006); **three to four nuclei, ever**. Og-294 half-life **0.7 ms**
- Decay chain: **²⁹⁴Og → ²⁹⁰Lv → ²⁸⁶Fl → ²⁸²Cn (spontaneous fission)**
- **Story beat:** named for **Yuri Oganessian, who was alive and on the call.** In a March 2016
  conference call among the discoverers of 115–118, they waited for Oganessian to leave the
  call, then unanimously agreed to name it after him. Official 28 Nov 2016.
- **Visual:** it sits in the noble gas column but **probably isn't one** — relativistic effects
  should make it far more reactive than radon, possibly with a *positive electron affinity*.
  End the table on the element that breaks the table.

## Animation data: decay chains

Two chains are fully characterized in the sources checked and are ready to animate as-is:

- **Nh-278:** 4 alphas → ²⁷⁴Rg → ²⁷⁰Mt → ²⁶⁶Bh → SF of ²⁶²Db
- **Og-294:** 3 alphas → ²⁹⁰Lv → ²⁸⁶Fl → SF of ²⁸²Cn

The rest need per-step alpha energies and per-step half-lives pulled from NUBASE2020 or the
IAEA chart before they can be timed accurately. **This is the one gap in this document** —
the IAEA nuclear data service was returning 502s during this pass. See Sourcing below.

## Sourcing

- Synthesis reactions, dates, labs, atom counts, naming histories, and half-lives above were
  taken from the Wikipedia articles for each element, which are cited to the Oganessian,
  Hofmann, Morita, and Ghiorso primary literature and to NUBASE.
- IUPAC's own announcement page for 113/115/117/118 confirms the labs, collaborations, and
  date ranges but carries **no** reaction or decay data.
- GSI's public "New Elements" page confirms which elements it discovered and when, but likewise
  carries no beam/target detail.

**Not yet verified against primary sources:** per-step alpha energies and per-step half-lives
for the decay chains, and the handful of half-lives flagged "unconfirmed" above
(Bh-278, Mt-282, Rg-286, Cn-285m, Fl-290, Lv-294). Anything that ends up as on-screen text
should be checked against NUBASE2020 (Kondev et al., *Chinese Physics C* **45**, 030001, 2021)
and the relevant IUPAC technical reports before it ships.
